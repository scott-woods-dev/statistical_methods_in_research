# Chapter 9: Logistic Regression

## 9.1 Introduction

Up to this point, we have primarily focused on continuous numerical outcomes. We built models to predict things like life expectancy or birth weight, where the response variable can take on a wide, continuous range of values. But what happens when our research question demands a simple binary answer? In many critical social and medical science applications, the response variable is a categorical event. A candidate wins an election, or they lose. A borrower defaults on a loan, or they pay it back. A patient survives a medical emergency, or they pass away.



To evaluate these situations, researchers use **logistic regression**. Unlike standard linear regression, which tries to fit a straight line to predict a specific numeric value, logistic regression is built to predict the *probability* that a specific category occurs. It essentially bends our standard straight line into a curve, ensuring that our model's predictions always make logical sense by staying strictly between 0% and 100%.



We will explore this method by stepping into a high-stakes clinical environment using the `icu_data.csv` dataset. An Intensive Care Unit handles the most severe, life-threatening cases in a hospital system, demanding continuous monitoring and rapid clinical intervention. Because critical care environments are high-risk, modeling patient survival alongside admission types and physiological baselines is central to improving triage and hospital resource management. 



Our motivating research question is straightforward: How do physiological indicators and the nature of a hospital admission influence a patient's probability of surviving to discharge?



We will rely on a random sample of 200 patients extracted from a larger clinical study to answer this question.

- **`Survive`**: The patient's survival status at hospital discharge ($1 = \text{the patient survived to be discharged}$, $0 = \text{the patient died in the hospital}$).
- **`Age`**: The age of the patient in years.
- **`Pulse`**: The patient's heart rate measured in beats per minute (bpm).
- **`Emergency`**: The type of ICU admission ($1 = \text{emergency admission}$, $0 = \text{elective admission}$).

By the end of this chapter, you will understand how software tools manage the complex math behind logistic regression, allowing us to focus entirely on translating the model's output into highly interpretable predicted probabilities. Furthermore, we will revisit the confusion matrix you learned about in the previous chapter. Instead of evaluating a simple, arbitrary rule like patient pulse rates, you will learn how to take the precise probabilities generated by a logistic regression model, set a decision threshold, and build a confusion matrix to accurately evaluate the model's true sensitivity and specificity.

## 9.2 The Core Concept: Moving Beyond 2x2 Tables

In the previous chapter, we analyzed relationships between categorical variables using tables. We could easily compare a patient's infection status (yes or no) against their survival status (yes or no). But what if we want to predict a **binary outcome** (an event with exactly two possible categorical results) using a continuous numerical variable?



Imagine we want to predict the probability of a patient surviving to discharge (`Survive`) based on their resting heart rate (`Pulse`). If we plot this data, the survival outcome on the y-axis can only exist at exactly 0 (died) or exactly 1 (survived).

```
[Insert Figure 9.1: Scatterplot showing Survive (0 or 1) on the y-axis and Pulse on the x-axis, featuring a standard linear regression line that crosses below 0 and above 1]
```

If we fit a standard linear regression line to this scatterplot, we run into an immediate logical problem. A straight line continues infinitely in both directions. For extremely high or low pulse rates, the straight line will eventually predict survival values greater than 1 or less than 0. Because we want to interpret these predictions as probabilities, a straight line will eventually claim a patient has a -20% or 115% chance of survival. This is mathematically impossible.



Standard linear regression fails when the outcome is a categorical yes or no result because straight lines do not respect the strict boundaries of probability. To solve this, researchers use a mathematical function that bends the straight line into an S-shaped curve. This curve flattens out as it approaches 0 and 1, ensuring that the model's predicted probabilities always remain valid, no matter how extreme the patient's pulse rate becomes.



## 9.3 The Math (Conceptually): The Logit

To mathematically create that S-shaped probability curve, we need a mathematical bridge. We build this bridge in two conceptual steps.



First, we translate probabilities into **odds**. Humans naturally think in probabilities, but to model our data, we need to compare the chance of an event happening to the chance of it *not* happening. If we let $\pi$ represent the true population probability of survival, the formula for odds is:


$$
\displaystyle \text{Odds} = \frac{\pi}{1 - \pi}
$$


For example, if a patient has an 80% probability of surviving ($\pi = 0.80$), the probability of not surviving is 20% ($1 - 0.80 = 0.20$). Dividing 0.80 by 0.20 gives us 4. The odds of survival are 4 to 1, or more directly, 4 patients survive for every 1 death.



While probabilities are neatly bounded between 0 and 1, odds are heavily skewed. Think about the "middle" of a probability scale, which is a 50/50 coin flip. If $\pi = 0.50$, the odds are exactly 1. This means every event less likely than a coin flip is compressed into the tiny mathematical space between 0 and 1. Meanwhile, every event more likely than a coin flip stretches from 1 all the way out to infinity. We cannot plug something this asymmetrical into a standard straight-line regression model.



To fix this severe skew, we use a mathematical stretching machine: the natural logarithm. In Chapter 4, you saw logarithms visually scale down heavy-sided economic data. Here, the logarithm acts geometrically. It takes that cramped 0-to-1 odds space and pulls it down toward negative infinity. It also takes the infinite stretch above 1 and scales it back down.  



This transformation centers our entire system perfectly at 0, creating a new, stretched metric called the **log-odds**, or the **logit**.

```
[Insert Figure 9.2: A three-panel progression. Panel A shows probability bounded between 0 and 1. Panel B shows odds, highlighting the severe compression below 1 and the infinite stretch above 1. Panel C shows the log-odds (logit) centered at 0, extending infinitely in both positive and negative directions.]
```

The logit gives us a canvas suitable for a straight regression line. When you run a logistic regression, the software uses your predictor variables to directly estimate this logit.



However, nobody communicates clinical risk using abstract log-odds. Because our regression estimates the logit directly, we will eventually have to reverse-transform these numbers to make useful, real-world predictions like probabilities. We will not worry about the math for that reverse transformation just yet. Our immediate next step is simply learning how to read the initial software output.



## 9.4 Interpreting the Output: Slopes and Odds Ratios

```
[Insert Table 9.1: Minitab logistic regression output table showing Constant and Age predictors, including raw Coef, SE, Z-Value, P-Value, and an Odds Ratio column]
```

When you run a logistic regression model, your software will output a familiar-looking table of coefficients. Because our model is predicting the logit, the raw slope we get back tells us how the log-odds change for every one-unit increase in our predictor variable.  



Because log-odds are incredibly abstract, researchers typically treat this raw slope strictly as a directional signal. Let's apply this to the `Age` variable in our dataset, which measures the patient's age in years. In our output table, the raw coefficient for age is -0.0275. The negative sign immediately tells us the direction of the relationship: as age increases, the log-odds of survival decrease. Older age is associated with lower survival rates.  

However, telling a doctor that a patient's log-odds of survival drop by 0.0275 each year is not practically useful. To make these numbers interpretable, statistical software automatically converts these raw slopes into an **Odds Ratio**.  



If we look at our software output, it translates that raw slope of -0.0275 into an Odds Ratio of approximately 0.97. This single number is highly interpretable. It tells us that for every one-year increase in a patient's age, their baseline odds of surviving are multiplied by a factor of 0.97.  



Why does multiplying by 0.97 matter? If you retain 100% of your baseline odds, your multiplier is exactly 1.00. By dropping to 0.97, you are retaining 97% of your baseline odds. Mathematically, this means you have lost 3% (1.00 - 0.97 = 0.03). Therefore, we can interpret this Odds Ratio as a 3% decrease in the *odds* of survival for every additional year of life.



We must be incredibly careful here: this is a 3% decrease in the *odds* of survival, not the *probability* of survival. Furthermore, because this is a multiplier, the risk compounds like interest on a bank account. A 3% decrease per year does not mean that after 10 years, a patient has lost a flat 30% of their odds. Instead, the odds shrink by 3% of the *previous year's* value, curving gently downward rather than dropping in a straight, predictable line. The Odds Ratio allows us to easily understand this compounding, multiplicative risk without ever having to calculate the complex underlying mathematics.

However, humans fundamentally think in percentages, not odds. If you are a triage nurse evaluating a 65-year-old patient, you do not want to calculate compounding odds; you want to know their actual percentage chance of surviving.  



To make our model truly actionable, we must convert our findings back into intuitive predicted probabilities for specific cases. Because our regression line was built using abstract log-odds, calculating the exact probability by hand requires complex mathematics. Instead of wrestling with those complex calculations, we can rely entirely on our statistical software tools to reverse-transform the model's output into a clean percentage.

## 9.7 Model Diagnostics and Accuracy: The Confusion Matrix

Our multiple logistic regression model provides precise predicted probabilities for patient survival. However, to evaluate how practically useful this model is in a high-stakes clinical setting, we need to see how well it actually classifies patients. To do this, researchers pick a probability threshold (commonly 50%) and instruct the software to make a hard prediction. If a patient's predicted probability of survival is over 50%, the model predicts they will survive. If it is under 50%, the model predicts they will die.



By cross-tabulating these predictions against what actually happened to the 200 patients in our sample, we build a confusion matrix.  

|                            | **Actual Death (0)** | **Actual Survival (1)** |
| -------------------------- | :------------------: | :---------------------: |
| **Predicted Death (0)**    |          2           |            1            |
| **Predicted Survival (1)** |          38          |           159           |

Table 9.7: Confusion Matrix cross-tabulating Predicted Survival against Actual Survival.



Looking at our matrix, we can categorize every patient into one of four groups:  

- **True Positives (159 patients):** The model predicted they would survive, and they actually survived.
- **True Negatives (2 patients):** The model predicted they would not survive, and they actually did not survive.
- **False Positives (38 patients):** The model predicted they would survive, but they unfortunately died.
- **False Negatives (1 patient):** The model predicted they would not survive, but they actually lived.

With these counts, we can calculate our model's accuracy using two metrics introduced in Chapter 8: sensitivity and specificity.  



Sensitivity measures the true positive rate: out of all the patients who *actually* survived, how many did our model successfully identify? In our sample, 160 patients survived in total (159 True Positives + 1 False Negative). Our model correctly predicted 159 of them. This gives our model a sensitivity of 99.4% (159 / 160).  



Specificity measures the true negative rate: out of all the patients who *actually* died, how many did our model correctly identify? In our sample, 40 patients died (2 True Negatives + 38 False Positives). Our model correctly flagged only 2 of them. This gives our model an abysmal specificity of 5.0% (2 / 40).  



While our model's $p$-values were statistically significant, this confusion matrix reveals a massive practical flaw. Because the baseline survival rate in this ICU dataset is relatively high, setting a 50% threshold causes our model to predict that almost *everyone* will survive. A model that cannot accurately identify high-risk patients (low specificity) is not particularly useful for hospital triage.  

### Adjusting the Threshold

Researchers are not permanently locked into a 50% threshold. In a medical setting, the cost of a false positive (predicting a patient will survive when they are actually at high risk of dying) is catastrophic. To fix our model's poor specificity, we could adjust the probability threshold upward. For instance, we might demand an 85% predicted probability before we classify someone in the "Predicted Survival" category.



By raising the bar for survival, more patients will fall into the "Predicted Death" bucket. This would correctly flag more high-risk patients, significantly improving our specificity. However, this is always a trade-off. By predicting death more often, we will inevitably flag some patients who would have survived just fine, lowering our sensitivity. Researchers must choose a threshold that carefully balances these ethical and practical stakes.

### The Illusion of $p$-values and Thoughtful Modeling

This massive gap between our significant $p$-values and our terrible specificity perfectly illustrates a core statistical truth: $p$-values only measure whether a relationship is better than random chance. A low $p$-value is strong evidence that a variable has *some* effect; it is absolutely not a measure of how useful the model is, how accurately it estimates reality, or whether it belongs in a clinical setting.



That is exactly why we rely on post-hoc diagnostics like the confusion matrix. Our two-variable model failed the practical test because human survival in an ICU is incredibly complex. A real-life triage model requires far more than just a patient's age and emergency status. Researchers must think carefully about the underlying science to select the right variables, perhaps incorporating vital signs like pulse or blood pressure (`Pulse`, `SysBP`), or existing infections (`Infection`), without adding so many variables that the model becomes convoluted overkill. Good modeling is driven by theory and evaluated by practical accuracy, not just by hunting for low $p$-values.  

## 9.8 Model Diagnostics and Accuracy: The ROC Curve and the AUC

In the previous section, we saw how manually adjusting our prediction threshold from 50% to 85% changed our model's sensitivity and specificity. But testing thresholds one by one is tedious. What if we wanted to see the accuracy trade-offs for *every* possible threshold between 0% and 100% all at once?



To do this, researchers generate a **Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve**.

```
[Insert Figure 9.3: ROC Curve for the multiple logistic regression model, plotting Sensitivity on the y-axis against 1 - Specificity on the x-axis]
```

An ROC curve is simply a visual summary of our model's performance across all possible decision thresholds. The vertical y-axis plots the Sensitivity (the True Positive Rate). The horizontal x-axis plots the False Positive Rate, which is simply 100% minus the Specificity.



When you look at an ROC curve, you will typically see a diagonal line cutting straight through the middle of the graph. This diagonal line represents a completely useless model, one that is effectively just flipping a coin. If our model is actually learning from the data, its curve will bow upward and away from that diagonal line, pushing toward the top-left corner of the graph. That top-left corner represents the holy grail of classification: 100% sensitivity and 0% false positives.

### Grading the Model: Area Under the Curve (AUC)

While the ROC curve provides a great visual, researchers usually want a single, standardized number to summarize how well the model distinguishes between categories. We get this by calculating the **Area Under the Curve (AUC)**.

Think of the AUC as a grading scale for your model's overall accuracy, ranging from 0.5 to 1.0.

- **0.50:** The model is guessing blindly (the diagonal line).
- **0.70 to 0.80:** The model is acceptable, but leaves significant room for improvement.
- **0.80 to 0.90:** The model is excellent.
- **1.00:** The model is perfect (which rarely happens in real-world data).

Conceptually, the AUC tells us the probability that our model will correctly rank two random observations. If we randomly pick one patient who actually survived and one patient who actually died, the AUC is the probability that our model assigns a higher predicted survival percentage to the patient who lived.



Our software output reports an Area Under the ROC Curve of 0.7491 for our two-variable model. This means our model has roughly a 75% chance of correctly distinguishing between a survivor and a non-survivor.



In a low-stakes social science survey, an AUC of 0.75 might be perfectly acceptable. However, in a high-stakes ICU environment, a "C-grade" model is not reliable enough for triage. This AUC value reinforces our conclusion from the confusion matrix: age and emergency status are important pieces of the puzzle, but they are not the entire picture.



## 9.9 Model Diagnostics and Accuracy: Deviance and Goodness of Fit

### Understanding Deviance

In Chapters 4 and 5, you learned to evaluate standard regression models by looking at residuals, which measure the physical distance between the actual data points and your line of best fit. Because logistic regression uses an S-shaped probability curve rather than a straight line, we cannot calculate standard residuals. Instead, logistic regression measures error using a concept called **deviance**.  



You can intuitively think of deviance as a measure of "badness of fit." A model with high deviance is making predictions that are very far away from the actual outcomes. A perfect model that predicts every single outcome correctly would have a deviance of zero. When we build a logistic regression model, our software is mathematically working behind the scenes to find the probability curve that results in the lowest possible deviance.

### Deviance $R^2$ and Adjusted Deviance $R^2$

With standard linear regression, we used $R^2$ to determine the percentage of variance in our outcome that was explained by our predictor variables. Because logistic regression involves binary outcomes, we cannot calculate variance in the exact same mathematical way. Instead, software provides a **Deviance $R^2$** (sometimes called a pseudo-$R^2$).  



While the underlying math is different, the conceptual interpretation is incredibly similar. Deviance $R^2$ tells us how much our model reduced the total error (the deviance) compared to a completely empty baseline model that uses no predictors at all.



Our model output reports a Deviance $R^2$ of 13.53%. This means that adding a patient's age and emergency admission status reduced the model's predictive error by roughly 13.5%. While this is an improvement over guessing blindly, it leaves a massive amount of deviance unexplained.



Furthermore, you may recall from Chapter 5 that adding *any* variable to a model, even a completely useless one, will mathematically force the standard $R^2$ to increase. To combat this, we use the Adjusted $R^2$, which applies a mathematical penalty for every variable you add to the model. The **Adjusted Deviance $R^2$** does the exact same thing for logistic regression. Our Adjusted Deviance $R^2$ drops slightly to 12.53%, reflecting the minor penalty applied for using two predictor variables.  

### The Deviance Goodness-of-Fit Test

As a final structural check, researchers often look at the **Deviance Goodness-of-Fit test**. This test formally evaluates whether the model's predicted probabilities adequately align with the actual observed outcomes.



We have to be very careful interpreting the $p$-value for this specific test because the hypotheses feel backward compared to what you are used to. In most tests, researchers are hunting for a small $p$-value to prove an effect exists. However, for a goodness-of-fit test:

- $H_0$: The model is a good fit for the data.
- $H_1$: The model is a poor fit for the data.  

Because the null hypothesis ($H_0$) assumes the model fits well, we actually *want* a large $p$-value here. A large $p$-value means we fail to reject $H_0$, indicating that there is no significant evidence that our model is poorly structured.  



Looking at our software output, the Deviance Goodness-of-Fit test yields a $p$-value of 0.890. Because 0.890 is much larger than a standard alpha level of 0.05, we fail to reject the null hypothesis. We have no evidence that the model is structurally flawed.



However, do not confuse a "good fit" with a "highly accurate model." The goodness-of-fit test simply confirms that the mathematical curve is behaving appropriately for the variables we provided. As we saw with our low 13.5% Deviance $R^2$ and our terrible 5% specificity from the confusion matrix, a model can be structurally sound while still missing the broader clinical reality of the intensive care unit.

## 9.10 Sample Sizes and Rare Events

In standard multiple regression, having a small sample size generally just gives us wider confidence intervals. Logistic regression, however, has a stricter requirement. Because we are predicting a categorical event, the total number of subjects ($n$) in your dataset is actually less important than the frequency of your least common outcome.  



Imagine we have a massive dataset of 1,000 ICU patients, but thanks to an incredible medical staff, only two patients died. If we try to build a logistic regression model, the software will mathematically struggle. It simply does not have enough examples of the "death" category to figure out what underlying pattern leads to it. Without enough data on the rare event, the algorithm will likely just guess that every single patient survives, rendering the model practically useless for triage.



As a general rule of thumb, researchers aim for at least 10 to 15 occurrences of the least common outcome for every predictor variable they include in the model. In our two-variable ICU model (`Age` and `Emergency`), we would need a minimum of 20 to 30 patient deaths in our dataset to generate reliable mathematical slopes. If your rare event is too infrequent, you cannot safely add more predictors. 

## 9.11 Beyond Binary Outcomes: Ordinal Regression

We built this chapter around binary outcomes, events with exactly two possibilities. But what happens if your research question involves an outcome with three or more categories that follow a natural sequence?



In the social sciences, researchers frequently use a **Likert scale** (such as "Disagree," "Neutral," and "Agree") to measure human attitudes. This creates an **ordinal** variable. The categories have a logical, ranked order, but the physical distance between them is unquantifiable. For example, the psychological jump from "Disagree" to "Neutral" might be much smaller than the jump from "Neutral" to "Agree."



Because the distances between the ranks are unknown, you cannot treat a single ordinal survey question as a continuous numerical variable in a standard linear regression. You also cannot use it in binary logistic regression because there are more than two possible choices.  



When you encounter this specific scenario in your own research, you need to use a tool called **ordinal logistic regression**. Standard statistical software packages include this tool specifically to handle ranked categories gracefully.



## 9.12 Communicating Results in APA Format

When writing up your findings for publication or research reports, you must combine your statistical output with clear, plain-English interpretations. Because logistic regression involves several moving parts, a complete APA-formatted summary needs to address the overall model fit, the significance of the individual predictors, the effect sizes (using Odds Ratios), and the model's practical predictive accuracy.



**Reporting the Overall Model**



Begin by stating the goal of the analysis and the variables used. Report the Deviance $R^2$ to indicate how much error the model reduced. If you reference the Deviance Goodness-of-Fit test, report the Chi-Square statistic ($\chi^2$), the degrees of freedom, and the $p$-value, reminding the reader that a non-significant $p$-value indicates a good structural fit.



**Reporting the Predictors**



For each predictor variable, you must report the test statistic (often a $z$-value in logistic regression) and the $p$-value to establish statistical significance. More importantly, you must report the Odds Ratio ($OR$) and its 95% Confidence Interval (CI) to communicate the practical effect size. Always translate the Odds Ratio into a plain-English percentage change or multiplier so the reader understands the real-world impact.



**Reporting Practical Accuracy**



Do not let your readers assume that significant $p$-values guarantee a highly accurate model. Always include the Area Under the Curve (AUC) to grade the model's overall discrimination. If you built a confusion matrix, report the threshold you used alongside the resulting sensitivity and specificity to demonstrate the model's limitations.

## 9.13 Common Mistakes / Misconceptions

1. **Interpreting an Odds Ratio as Relative Risk:** We must never say an emergency patient is "four times less likely" to survive based on an Odds Ratio. An Odds Ratio is a multiplier applied strictly to the *odds*. Conflating odds with standard probability (Relative Risk) is one of the most common errors in published research.



2. **Trusting the $p$-value over the Confusion Matrix:** A highly significant $p$-value only tells you a predictor is performing better than random chance. It absolutely does not mean the model is practically accurate or useful for real-world classification, as we saw with our model's terrible specificity.

   

3. **Forgetting to check for outliers:** In standard linear regression, you always start with a scatterplot, which makes extreme outliers on the x-axis or y-axis immediately obvious. Because logistic regression forces the y-axis to be exactly 0 or 1, standard scatterplots look like two parallel horizontal lines of dots. This visual format makes outliers incredibly hard to spot. Researchers often forget to check their continuous predictors (like `Pulse` or `SysBP`) for extreme values before running the model, allowing a single extreme patient to invisibly warp the S-shaped probability curve. 



## 9.14 Key Concepts

1. **Logistic Regression for Binary Outcomes:** When a response variable has exactly two categorical outcomes (like surviving or dying), standard linear regression fails because its straight line will eventually predict impossible values outside the 0% to 100% probability bounds.
2. **The Logit Bridge:** To use regression math on a binary outcome, we translate probabilities into odds, and then use the natural logarithm to create the **logit** (log-odds). This transformation centers the data at zero and stretches it infinitely in both directions, allowing the software to fit a model.
3. **Interpreting Odds Ratios:** Raw regression slopes tell us the direction of a relationship, but statistical software converts these abstract numbers into highly interpretable Odds Ratios. An Odds Ratio acts as a compounding multiplicative factor, telling us how a one-unit increase in a predictor shrinks or grows the baseline odds of the outcome.
4. **Predicted Probabilities:** The ultimate practical goal of a logistic model is translating the abstract logit back into an intuitive percentage. We rely entirely on software to generate these predicted probabilities for specific real-world profiles (e.g., a 65-year-old elective admission).
5. **Evaluating Practical Accuracy:** A statistically significant $p$-value only proves a variable is performing better than random chance. To evaluate real-world usefulness, we pick a probability threshold, build a confusion matrix, and calculate the model's true Sensitivity and Specificity.
6. **ROC Curves and AUC:** The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve visualizes a model's trade-off between Sensitivity and False Positives across all possible decision thresholds. The Area Under the Curve (AUC) provides a single numerical grade (from 0.5 to 1.0) summarizing the model's overall ability to distinguish between the two outcomes.
7. **Deviance and Fit:** Logistic regression measures predictive error using deviance rather than standard residuals. Deviance $R^2$ estimates the percentage reduction in error compared to a blind guess, while the Deviance Goodness-of-Fit test formally evaluates the structural fit of the curve (where a large $p$-value means we fail to reject the assumption that the model fits well).
8. **The Rare Events Rule:** Having a massive total sample size is not enough for logistic regression. The model specifically requires a sufficient number of occurrences of the least common outcome (roughly 10 to 15 occurrences per predictor variable) to calculate reliable slopes.
