2  Proportions and Reliability

Chapter 2 of the textbook shows how the outcome variable decides the statistical measure: a yes/no outcome calls for a proportion. It also introduces Cronbach’s alpha, the check that lets you add survey items into one score. In this chapter you will calculate proportions, for one group and for two groups side by side, then calculate Cronbach’s alpha and build the combined score.

Get the script: Download the Chapter 2 script, chapter_02.R, and save it in the same folder as your data.

2.1 The data

This chapter uses two datasets. Download both into the same folder as the script:

  • Download youth_risk_2007.csv, survey answers from 13,387 American high school students about riding with a driver who had been drinking.
  • Download amas_math_anxiety.csv, answers from 2,200 adults to the nine-item Abbreviated Math Anxiety Scale (AMAS), the scale used in the textbook chapter.

The variables used in this chapter:

Variables used in this chapter
Variable Dataset What it records

2.3 A proportion for one variable

A proportion is the share of observations in one category: the count in that category divided by the total. Start by counting with table():

# 1. COUNT AND PROPORTION FOR ONE VARIABLE.
#    table() counts how many rows have each value. Here 0 means
#    "no" and 1 means "yes, rode with a driver who had been
#    drinking".
# CHANGE THIS: your categorical variable after the $
ride_counts <- table(youth$ride.alc.driver)
ride_counts

   0    1 
9210 4177 
# prop.table() turns the counts into proportions (each count
# divided by the total), so the proportions add up to 1.
# round(..., 3) rounds them to 3 decimal places.
round(prop.table(ride_counts), 3)

    0     1 
0.688 0.312

The first table gives the counts: 9,210 students answered 0 (no) and 4,177 answered 1 (yes). The second gives the proportions: 0.312, or about 31.2%, of these students rode with a driver who had been drinking.

The names on top of each table (0 and 1) are the codes stored in the data. Check the dataset’s key to see what each code means.

For a variable coded 0 and 1, the mean is the same as the proportion of 1s, which gives you a one-line shortcut:

# Shortcut for a variable coded 0 and 1: its mean is the
# proportion of 1s.
mean(youth$ride.alc.driver)
[1] 0.3120191
TipWith your own data

Change ride.alc.driver to your categorical variable. table() works for any number of categories and for categories stored as words, such as Bus, Car, and Walk. The mean() shortcut works only for a variable coded 0 and 1; if it gives NA, add na.rm = TRUE as in Chapter 1.

2.4 Proportions for two groups

To compare a proportion across groups, count every combination of the two variables. Put the group variable first (it becomes the rows) and the outcome second (it becomes the columns):

# 2. PROPORTIONS FOR TWO GROUPS.
#    table(rows, columns) counts every combination of two
#    variables. The first variable becomes the rows and the second
#    becomes the columns. Here smoke is 0 = never tried smoking,
#    1 = has tried smoking.
# CHANGE THIS: your group variable first, then your outcome
ride_by_smoke <- table(youth$smoke, youth$ride.alc.driver)
ride_by_smoke
   
       0    1
  0 4940 1113
  1 4027 2919
# margin = 1 divides each row by its own row total, so each row
# adds up to 1. That gives the proportion of "yes" answers within
# each group.
round(prop.table(ride_by_smoke, margin = 1), 3)
   
        0     1
  0 0.816 0.184
  1 0.580 0.420

In the first table, the rows are smoke (0 = never tried smoking, 1 = has tried smoking) and the columns are ride.alc.driver (0 = no, 1 = yes). In the second table, each row adds up to 1. Read across a row: among students who had never tried smoking, 0.184 (18.4%) rode with a driver who had been drinking; among students who had tried smoking, 0.420 (42.0%) did.

WarningCommon error: the rows do not add up to 1

What you see

round(prop.table(ride_by_smoke, margin = 2), 3)
   
        0     1
  0 0.551 0.276
  1 0.449 0.724

What it means: margin = 2 divides by the column totals, so each column adds up to 1 instead of each row. These numbers answer a different question (“of the students who rode with a drinking driver, what share had smoked?”).

How to fix it: use margin = 1, and put the group variable first inside table(), so each row is one group.

NoteBlank cells are left out

table() skips blank cells without telling you. Here 388 students left smoke blank, so the two-group table counts 12,999 students instead of 13,387. Add the counts in a table to see how many rows it used.

TipWith your own data

Put your group variable first and your outcome variable second inside table(), and keep margin = 1.

2.5 Cronbach’s alpha

Cronbach’s alpha checks whether a set of survey items measures one thing consistently enough to add the items together. Read the anxiety data, then pick out the items that belong to one scale. The AMAS has two parts; this example uses the five items on anxiety about learning math.

# Read the math anxiety survey and store it under the name
# "anxiety".
# CHANGE THIS: the file name of your dataset, inside the quotes.
anxiety <- read.csv("amas_math_anxiety.csv")
names(anxiety)
[1] "item_1" "item_2" "item_3" "item_4" "item_5" "item_6" "item_7"
[8] "item_8" "item_9"
# 3. CRONBACH'S ALPHA.
#    First, pick out the survey items that belong to one scale.
#    Square brackets choose part of a dataset: [rows, columns].
#    Leaving the space before the comma empty means "all rows".
#    c() combines the column names into one list.
#    Here we use the five "learning anxiety" items.
# CHANGE THIS: the names of your items, each inside quotes
learning_items <- anxiety[, c("item_1", "item_3", "item_6",
                              "item_7", "item_9")]
head(learning_items)
  item_1 item_3 item_6 item_7 item_9
1      5      5      5      5      5
2      2      2      2      2      2
3      1      1      1      1      1
4      1      2      2      4      4
5      2      1      1      1      3
6      2      2      2      2      2

The square brackets choose part of a dataset. Inside them, the space before the comma picks rows and the part after the comma picks columns. Leaving the row part empty keeps every row, so anxiety[, c("item_1", "item_3")] means “all rows, just these two columns.”

WarningCommon error: object ‘item_1’ not found

What you see

anxiety[, c(item_1, item_3)]
Error:
! object 'item_1' not found

What it means: without quotation marks, R looks for something stored under the name item_1, finds nothing, and stops.

How to fix it: put each column name inside quotation marks, as in c("item_1", "item_3").

R prints a lot. The two places to look:

  • The first table, under raw_alpha: Cronbach’s alpha for the whole set of items. Here it is 0.91, above 0.70, the value researchers generally look for before they add items into one score.
  • Reliability if an item is dropped, under raw_alpha: what alpha would be with that one item removed. If removing an item would raise alpha noticeably, that item does not fit well with the others. Here every value is 0.91 or lower, so every item helps.

You can ignore the other columns.

WarningCommon error: Unknown colour name

What you see (the list of numbers is much longer)

Error: Unknown colour name: c(5, 2, 1, 1, 2, 2, 4, 1, 1, 1, ...

What it means: you wrote alpha() without psych:: in front, and R used the alpha() from ggplot2, which sets how see-through a color is. It has nothing to do with Cronbach’s alpha.

How to fix it: write psych::alpha(), as in the script.

WarningCommon error: items were negatively correlated

What you see

# A copy of the items with item_1 flipped, so 1 becomes 5 and
# 5 becomes 1, to show the warning.
flipped_items <- learning_items
flipped_items$item_1 <- 6 - flipped_items$item_1
psych::alpha(flipped_items)
Warning in psych::alpha(flipped_items): Some items were negatively correlated with the first principal component and probably 
should be reversed.  
To do this, run the function again with the 'check.keys=TRUE' option

What it means: one item runs in the opposite direction from the others. This happens when a survey includes items worded the other way (for example, “I feel calm during math tests”), where a high score means low anxiety. Left as is, the item pulls alpha down (in this example, to about 0.59).

How to fix it: reverse the item before calculating alpha. For a 1 to 5 scale, subtract each answer from 6, as in items$item_1 <- 6 - items$item_1, so 1 becomes 5 and 5 becomes 1. For a 1 to 7 scale, subtract from 8.

TipWith your own data

Put your item names inside c(), each in quotation marks, and give the set a name that describes it. Report the raw_alpha value from the first table.

2.6 Composite score

Once alpha shows the items fit together, add each person’s answers into one score. rowSums() adds across each row, and the <- arrow stores the result as a new column in the dataset:

# 4. COMPOSITE SCORE: add each person's answers across the items.
#    rowSums() adds across each row. The <- arrow stores the
#    result as a new column called learning_score in the dataset.
# CHANGE THIS: a name for your new score after the $, and the name
#              of your items inside rowSums()
anxiety$learning_score <- rowSums(learning_items)

# Check the new scores: the first six, then the mean.
head(anxiety$learning_score)
[1] 25 10  5 13  8 10
mean(anxiety$learning_score)
[1] 8.907727

Each person now has a learning anxiety score from 5 (an answer of 1 on all five items) to 25 (an answer of 5 on all five). The average is about 8.9. The new column works like any other: use anxiety$learning_score in mean(), or put learning_score in a ggplot() histogram from Chapter 1.

TipWith your own data

Choose a name for the new score after the $, and put the items you checked with alpha inside rowSums(). If any item has blank cells, rowSums() gives NA for those people; add na.rm = TRUE only if your study’s instructions say to score people with missing answers.
